Lesson 1 — VSEPR Theory & Electron Domains

The Big Idea

  • Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion (VSEPR): regions of electron density around a central atom repel each other
  • Domains spread out to be as far apart as possible — this minimizes repulsion
  • A domain can be a bonding pair OR a lone pair — both count

The arrangement that minimizes repulsion between all domains is called the electron-domain geometry.

Counting Electron Domains

  • Look only at the central atom
  • Count: (# of bonding domains) + (# of lone pairs)
  • A double or triple bond still counts as ONE domain — it's one region of electron density, just a denser one
⚠ Lone pairs take up MORE room than bonding pairs — they push harder on their neighbors and squeeze the remaining angles smaller.

Electron-Domain Geometry Names

  • 2 domains → Linear
  • 3 domains → Trigonal planar
  • 4 domains → Tetrahedral
  • 5 domains → Trigonal bipyramidal
  • 6 domains → Octahedral

Lesson 2 — Molecular Geometry & Hybridization

Molecular Geometry ≠ Electron-Domain Geometry

  • Molecular geometry is the shape you'd actually see — lone pairs are invisible, but they still push
  • Same domain count, different molecular geometry depending on how many domains are lone pairs
  • Example: 4 domains → tetrahedral (0 LP), trigonal pyramidal (1 LP), or bent (2 LP)

Hybridization Quick Map

  • sp — 2 domains, 180°
  • sp² — 3 domains, 120°
  • sp³ — 4 domains, 109.5°
  • sp³d — 5 domains, 90°/120°
  • sp³d² — 6 domains, 90°
Hybridization describes the central atom's own orbitals — always read off the TOTAL domain count, not the molecular geometry name.

Sigma (σ) vs. Pi (π) Bonds

  • σ bond: direct, end-to-end orbital overlap. Present in every single, double, and triple bond.
  • π bond: sideways overlap of unhybridized p orbitals. Only in double/triple bonds, IN ADDITION to a σ bond.
  • A π bond blocks free rotation → double bonds can have cis/trans (geometric) isomers

Worked Example — Predicting Shape from a Lewis Structure

Species: ozone, O₃, and the trichlorostannate ion, SnCl₃⁻

O₃ (one resonance form)
SnCl₃⁻
Step 1 — Count domains on the central atomO₃: the central O is bonded to two outer O's (one by a single bond, one by a double bond, in any one resonance form) and carries one lone pair → 3 domains total.
SnCl₃⁻: the central Sn is bonded to three Cl atoms and carries one lone pair → 4 domains total.
Step 2 — Electron-domain geometryO₃: 3 domains → trigonal planar arrangement.
SnCl₃⁻: 4 domains → tetrahedral arrangement.
Step 3 — Remove the lone pair(s) to get molecular geometryO₃: one of the three domains is a lone pair, so the visible shape is bent (angle just under 120°).
SnCl₃⁻: one of the four domains is a lone pair, so the visible shape is trigonal pyramidal (angle just under 109.5°).
Lesson 3 — Molecular Polarity

The Big Idea

A bond can be polar without the whole molecule being polar. Whether a molecule has a net dipole moment depends on whether the individual bond dipoles cancel out geometrically.

Bond polarity is about electronegativity difference. Molecular polarity is about geometry. You need both to answer a polarity question.

When Dipoles Cancel (Nonpolar)

  • Linear, identical atoms on both ends — e.g. CO₂
  • Trigonal planar, identical substituents, NO lone pair on the central atom — e.g. BF₃
  • Tetrahedral, identical substituents — e.g. CH₄, CCl₄
  • Square planar or octahedral, identical substituents — e.g. XeF₄, SF₆

When Dipoles Don't Cancel (Polar)

  • Any lone pair on the central atom almost always breaks the symmetry — e.g. NH₃, H₂O, SO₂
  • Mixed substituents on an otherwise symmetric shape — e.g. CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃
  • Any diatomic molecule of two different elements — e.g. BrCl, HF
Rule of thumb: a lone pair on the central atom → almost always polar.

Checklist for Any Molecule

  • 1. Draw the Lewis structure (with lone pairs)
  • 2. Determine the molecular geometry
  • 3. Ask: do the individual bond dipoles point in directions that cancel by symmetry?
  • 4. If yes → nonpolar. If no → polar.

Lesson 4 — Resonance & Bond Order

What Resonance Really Means

  • Some molecules/ions need more than one valid Lewis structure to be drawn
  • The real molecule is NOT flipping back and forth between them
  • The real structure is an average — a resonance hybrid — of all contributing structures

Bond Order

Bond order formulaBond order = (total bonds between two atoms, summed across all resonance structures) / (number of resonance structures)
  • A single bond only in every structure → bond order 1
  • Double in some structures, single in others → bond order between 1 and 2

Bond Order → Length & Energy

  • Higher bond order → shorter, stronger bond
  • Lower bond order → longer, weaker bond
  • Resonance-averaged bonds are all the SAME length — not a mix of long and short
This is why the two C–O bonds in a carboxylate group, or all three N–O bonds in nitrate, measure identical lengths experimentally.
Worked Example — Comparing Bond Order in Nitrate and Nitrite

Nitrate, NO₃⁻: three equivalent resonance structures, each with one N=O double bond and two N–O single bonds.

Nitrate, NO₃⁻ (3 resonance structures)
Nitrite, NO₂⁻ (2 resonance structures)
Step 1 — Total N–O bonds across all structuresEach structure contributes 1 double bond (counts as 2) + 2 single bonds (count as 1 each) = 4 bond-units, spread over 3 resonance structures and 3 equivalent bonds.
Step 2 — Bond orderBond order = 4 total bond-units ÷ 3 bonds = 1.33 for each N–O bond.

Nitrite, NO₂⁻: two equivalent resonance structures, each with one N=O double bond and one N–O single bond.

Step 3 — Bond order(2 + 1) bond-units ÷ 2 bonds = 1.5 for each N–O bond.
Step 4 — Compare1.5 > 1.33, so nitrite's N–O bonds have the HIGHER bond order → they are SHORTER and STRONGER than nitrate's N–O bonds.
